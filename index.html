      How it works

      What the number means

      Your phone's motion sensor feels every shake. The page weights them with the comfort weightings published in ISO 2631-1:1997, the international standard on whole-body vibration: people feel up-and-down shaking most at about 4 to 12 times a second, and side-to-side or front-to-back swaying most at about once or twice a second. The result is one number in m/s² (metres per second squared). The standard gives a rough guide to how passengers react to it; these are its descriptions:

      m/s²ISO's description
      under 0.315Not uncomfortable
      0.315 to 0.63A little uncomfortable
      0.5 to 1Fairly uncomfortable
      0.8 to 1.6Uncomfortable
      1.25 to 2.5Very uncomfortable
      over 2Extremely uncomfortable

      The ranges overlap on purpose, because people differ. Where two overlap, this page switches label halfway through the overlap. Comfort is about much more than vibration (noise, temperature, the seat, how you sit), and none of that is measured here.

      Where to put the phone

      Fix it in place so it can't slide or fall: in a phone holder or mount (on the dashboard, an air vent, an armrest or a tray table), or a snug seat-back pocket. Any way up is fine: the page works out which way is down. It feels whatever it's fixed to, and a holder adds a little give of its own, so a dashboard holder and a seat-back pocket give different numbers for the same ride: compare rides with the same phone in the same holder or spot. Loose on your lap or in your hand, your body softens the shaking, so those numbers don't compare either. If you're driving, start it before you set off and leave it alone. On a bike, clip it firmly into a handlebar mount: it then feels the handlebars rather than the saddle, so compare bike rides with each other, and quick leans into corners may be left out like a pick-up. Picking the phone up spoils the reading, so those seconds are left out automatically, as is any time without readings, plus a couple of seconds after each break while the calculation settles.

      Measuring stops by itself after two hours, or if the readings stop for five minutes.

      Seasickness

      ISO 2631-1 also gives a motion-sickness dose for slow up-and-down swaying, the kind boats make, and a rough rule for how many people that much motion would make vomit. It builds up over time, so it means most on rides of 20 minutes or more. Car sickness comes mostly from braking and cornering, which this dose doesn't count.

      Limits

      This is a bit of fun, not a measuring instrument. Your phone hasn't been calibrated or checked against a reference sensor, and phones differ, so compare rides from the same phone placed the same way rather than across phones. It measures one spot and only straight-line shaking, not rotation. "Up and down" means along gravity, so in a reclined seat or a vehicle that banks or pitches the split between up-and-down and sideways is approximate. Phones typically report motion about 60 times a second (each ride's technical details show the real rate): shaking faster than about half that rate isn't measured, and can even show up as slower shaking. The standard covers shaking up to 80 times a second. Jolt sizes are approximate, and a sensor can top out on a big knock. The standard itself is being revised. Never use these numbers for safety, health or work-exposure decisions.

      Privacy

      Everything is worked out on your phone. Nothing is uploaded, no location is used, and there are no cookies or analytics. Saved rides stay in this browser, and you can delete them all from the list of your rides.

      Questions

      How do I find out how bumpy my ride was?

      Choose what you're riding and tap Start, then fix your phone in place within three seconds, for example in a phone holder or a seat-back pocket. Leave it there and tap Stop when you arrive. You get a verdict, a timeline of the ride, the biggest jolts and a seasickness dose.

      Can I use it on a plane to see how bumpy the flight was?

      Yes. Tap Start and fix the phone in place, for example in the seat-back pocket or a holder on the tray table, following the crew's instructions for phones during take-off and landing. Once the page is open it keeps working in flight mode. Turbulence shows up as up-and-down and sideways shaking, the same as a rough road. It's just for fun: it can't tell you anything about how safe a flight is.

      Is it accurate?

      It's a fun estimate. It weights the shaking with the comfort weightings from ISO 2631-1:1997, but a phone isn't a calibrated instrument, phones differ, and it measures one spot. It's best for comparing your own rides, with the same phone in the same spot.

      Can I use it on a bike?

      Yes: choose Bike and clip the phone firmly into a handlebar mount. It feels the handlebars rather than the saddle, so it's best for comparing bike rides with each other, such as which path or road is smoother. Quick leans into corners may be left out, like picking the phone up.

      Does it work on iPhone and Android?

      Yes, in Safari, Chrome, Firefox or Edge. An iPhone asks for permission to use motion the first time you tap Start: tap Allow. Most laptops have no motion sensor, so try the sample ride there instead.

      Does it work without signal?

      Yes. Open it once with a connection, and after that it loads and measures without one: in tunnels, at sea or in flight mode. To keep it handy, add it to your home screen: in Safari tap Share, then Add to Home Screen; in Chrome, choose Install app or Add to Home screen from the menu.
